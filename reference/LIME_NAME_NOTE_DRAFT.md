The proposal said *"the name of the leaves was there before the word got offensive."* The record
runs the other way:

| date | what is documented |
|---|---|
| centuries pre-1800 | Arabic *kāfir* ("unbeliever") carried down the East African coast by slavers and applied to non-Muslim Africans |
| **1885** | Whitworth's *Anglo-Indian Dictionary* records the word as **"a common term of abuse"** |
| **1888** | **Earliest known reference to the LIME**, Emanuel Bonavia, *The Cultivated Oranges, Lemons Etc. of India and Ceylon*: *"Europeans call it Caffre Lime."* |

⚖️ **So the abuse is documented BEFORE the fruit name, not after.** Publishing the reverse would be
a **potato bobotie (Law 43)** on the one topic where being wrong is least forgivable — and it is
the kind of claim a reader checks in ten seconds.

✅ **THE TRUE VERSION IS A BETTER ARGUMENT ANYWAY**, and it is what the note below uses:
**the fruit was never named after South Africa.** The likely route is the **Sri Lanka Kaffirs**, a
community partly descended from enslaved Bantu Africans; Sri Lankans called the same fruit the
**leech lime**, because the crushed leaves were smeared on the legs to keep land leeches off in the
plantations. That is a genuine moat *(register: etymology + colonial botany)* and it is true.

## 📝 THE NOTE — FULL FORM *(one place, linked from the ingredient)*

> **Why this leaf has three names.**
> The tree is *Citrus hystrix*, and it comes from Southeast Asia. In Thailand it is **makrut**; in
> Cambodia *sleuk kroche*; in Indonesia *jeruk purut*. In most South African shops you will see it
> labelled **Thai lime**, and in older cookbooks and some packets, **kaffir lime.**
>
> That last word is the one worth being straight about. It comes from the Arabic *kāfir*, meaning
> unbeliever, and it reached this fruit a long way from here: the earliest record of the name is an
> 1888 survey of the citrus of India and Ceylon, which notes that Europeans in Sri Lanka called it
> the caffre lime. Sri Lankans had their own name for it — the **leech lime** — because the crushed
> leaves were rubbed on the legs to keep land leeches off in the plantations. The name most likely
> travelled through the Sri Lanka Kaffirs, a community partly descended from enslaved Bantu
> Africans.
>
> **So the fruit was never named after South Africa.** But the same Arabic word had already been
> carried down the East African coast by slavers, and by the 1880s it was in use here as a slur —
> which it remains, and which is why much of the cooking world now writes makrut, and why our shops
> mostly say Thai lime.
>
> We list all three names so that you can find the leaves whatever the packet says. We use the one
> you are most likely to see on a shelf in South Africa. And we would rather write this paragraph
> than pretend the question does not come up.
