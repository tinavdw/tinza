**THE LEAD — the ginger you can actually buy, and the one step no other pickle here uses:**

- **Young ginger (shin shoga) is what Japan makes this with**: dug early, sold in summer with pink-tipped
  stems, pale, juicy, mild, nearly fibre-free. **Mature ginger is what is on the shelf everywhere else** —
  the same rhizome months older, so the heat has built and **long tough fibres have developed**. Both make
  gari. They do not make the same gari, and the adjustments are given rather than pretended away.
- ⚠️ **SLICE ACROSS THE FIBRES, NEVER ALONG** — the single most important cut in the record. The fibres run
  the length of the rhizome, in the same direction as the knobbly branches. With the grain, every piece is
  a bundle of threads that will not part in the mouth *no matter how thin*.
- **1mm, thin enough to see light through**, and this is one of the very few places a mandoline earns its
  keep — not for neatness, but because gari is eaten by the piece, uncooked, and 1mm dissolves against the
  tongue where 3mm has to be chewed twice. A peeler dragged down a peeled knob is the fallback.
- **Salt 20 min and squeeze** — the salt-and-pressure argument is the tsukemono record's and is **pointed
  at, not repeated**. The discarded liquid carries the harshest of the raw heat with it.
- ⚖️ **THEN BLANCH 30 SECONDS — the step that separates this from every other pickle in the file.** Almost
  nothing in the Japanese pickle repertoire goes near boiling water, and this does for two reasons:
  gingerol's aggressive edge comes off while the aroma stays, *which is why blanched gari is refreshing
  rather than fierce*; and the fibre softens enough for a thin slice to **bend rather than snap**, opening
  the flesh so it takes the vinegar in hours instead of days. **A minute too long and it is cooked ginger,
  which goes soft and never recovers.**
- **Sweet vinegar mixed cold**, ~2.5 parts vinegar to 1 sugar — sharper than a cucumber dressing **because
  the acid is what makes it keep a month.** ⚠️ **Warm drained ginger into COLD vinegar, not the reverse**:
  the residual heat pulls the liquid in as it cools, so the jar seasons itself in the first ten minutes.
  Submerged, 24 hours minimum, better at a week.

**MOAT = THE COLOUR, AND IT RUNS BACKWARDS FROM WHAT EVERYONE ASSUMES.** Real gari from young summer
ginger turns a faint blush pink **entirely on its own** — the pale flesh and the pink-tinged skin near the
stem carry natural pigments that shift colour in acid, the same behaviour that turns shredded red cabbage
bright pink the moment vinegar hits it. **Nobody adds anything, and nobody ever did.** But that blush
cannot survive industrial production: mature ginger has no pigment to shift, bulk pickling washes out what
little there is, and a cream-yellow slice looks like nothing in a plastic tub under a light. So the trade
colours it. **The inversion: the bright pink ginger most of the world thinks is authentic is the industrial
version, and the almost-colourless one is the real one.** Second thread, older: ginger has measurable
antibacterial activity and a strongly acidic pickle adds a second layer of it — so in the centuries before
refrigeration, when sushi was street food sold from a stall in a hot city, **a mouthful between pieces was
a sensible precaution, not a garnish.** It survived into the modern meal as an etiquette whose reason the
fridge had quietly solved — which is why the heap sits to one side and never on the fish.
