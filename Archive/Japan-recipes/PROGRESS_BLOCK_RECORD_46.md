**THE LEAD IS THE BUYING AND THE ICE, exactly as briefed — the knife gets nine lines and a
pointer to Nigirizushi.** What the record actually teaches:

- **"Sashimi grade" means nothing.** No grade, no inspection, no certificate. It describes intent.
- **Safety is cold, not freshness.** ~-20°C for 7 days through, or ~-35°C for 15–20 hrs, is the
  international parasite rule. A home freezer runs near -18°C and cycles every time it opens, so
  **you cannot do this at home** — which produces the counter-intuitive rule the card is built on:
  **for raw eating, PREVIOUSLY FROZEN BEATS NEVER FROZEN.** The most useful sentence at a counter is
  *"has this been frozen?"*
- **Two routes open, one closed.** Commercially frozen tuna block, thawed at home ✅ · farmed
  pellet-fed salmon ✅ · the beautiful wild fish landed this morning and never frozen ❌ — wild fish is
  where the parasites are. Stated plainly, and the budget version repeats it so nobody swaps down.
- **Five things you can check standing there:** buy a **BLOCK not slices** (a fraction of the exposed
  surface, and you can read the grain) · smell = clean seawater or nothing, ammonia = walk away ·
  gloss and translucency, not matte and gaping · what it is **sitting in** (milky pool = broken flesh
  from bad handling or a bad freeze-thaw) · **be suspicious of an impossibly even bright cherry red** —
  carbon-monoxide / "tasteless smoke" treatment locks the pigment and hides age; not permitted in
  Japan or the EU, permitted elsewhere. Real tuna dulls as it ages, and **that is a feature.**
- **The thaw is half the quality:** 8–12 hrs in the coldest part of the fridge, on a rack so it never
  lies in its own drip. Never water, never microwave, never counter. Slow-thawed fish reabsorbs its
  own melt; fast-thawed fish leaves it on the plate and goes spongy. **Never refreeze.**
- **THE ICE, and the detail most photographs get wrong: the ice NEVER touches the fish.** Chilled
  plate ON the ice tray — meltwater against the flesh blanches and waterlogs the surface in minutes.
  Cold board, cold blade, and **15 min in the freezer to firm the block** before cutting (firming the
  surface, not refreezing it).
- **The daikon bed (tsuma) earns its place three times:** holds the fish off the cold plate so it
  chills from below without sitting in water · absorbs drip · resets the palate between two fish.
- **Serving laws:** wasabi as a dab **on the plate**, never stirred into the soy (drowns a heat that
  fades within ~15 min of grating) · 20ml of soy and dip **one edge of one face** · gari **between**
  the fish, never on it · lean fish first, fat fish after · **on the table within 15 minutes.**
- **Two non-negotiables, stated straight:** own board, own knife, own patch of counter, nowhere near
  raw chicken · and raw fish is not the dish for anyone pregnant, very young, elderly and frail, or
  immunocompromised — cook their portion, nobody minds being asked.

**MOAT = the cold chain, and it is a status-inversion story, not a name origin.** Tuna as
**neko-matagi** — the fish even a cat steps over — cheap Edo-period labourers' fish whose fatty belly
was near worthless *because fat spoils fastest and nothing could hold it*. Then two mechanical steps:
Japanese longliners from the 1960s carrying **ultra-low-temperature freezers at around -50 to -60°C**,
cold enough that the fat itself stops changing and a fish holds most of a year (which is why the best
tuna has almost always been frozen); then 1970s cargo flights leaving Japan **half empty**, filled on
the return leg with Atlantic bluefin that North American crews had been selling for very little,
sometimes for pet food. A market that would pay real money built itself in a decade.

Daigaku Imo's "university potato" is still the unspent fifth.
⛔ **Not reused:** negitoro/toro-thrift (spent in Hosomaki) and **Norway's Project Japan salmon
campaign (spent in Oshizushi's Sake version)** — checked by grepping every trivia field before writing.
