**THE LEAD — two things decide this dish and neither is the dressing:**

1. **THE ACCORDION CUT (jabara), and it is taught properly** because it is the reason to make this
   rather than read about it. **A chopstick or wooden spoon handle laid tight against each side of the
   cucumber as STOPS** — that is what makes it foolproof — then a shallow, almost-lying-down blade,
   2–3mm cuts, letting the knife come to rest on the chopsticks so it *cannot* go through. Then turn it
   over and repeat, so the two sets of cuts lean towards each other and meet in the middle. It stretches
   like a spring. **Why:** a plain round has two flat faces and carries dressing on the outside, so it
   tastes of vinegar outside and of nothing in the middle; the accordion is many times that surface and
   **all of it interior**, while the uncut spine keeps every piece rigid. *Flavour and crunch normally
   pull against each other in a salad — this cut is how you get both, and the same six ingredients taste
   completely different on knife work alone.*
2. ⚠️ **DRESS IT THIRTY SECONDS BEFORE IT REACHES THE TABLE.** Vinegar and salt keep drawing water for
   as long as they are touching the cucumber, so a salad dressed early goes limp *and* dilutes its own
   dressing into pink water. **You cannot squeeze a dressed salad** — there is no recovery. Which is why
   this is made in the ten minutes before eating and never in the get-ahead cooking, and why a
   restaurant keeps cucumber, wakame and dressing in three separate containers until the order lands.

Plus the honest adjustment for the cucumber actually on the shelf: the Japanese variety is slim,
thin-skinned and nearly seedless, so **a big watery one gets halved and the soft seed core scraped out
with a teaspoon before cutting** — that core is water with no crunch in it and is the commonest reason a
sunomono ends in a puddle. Deseed, then cut, then salt. And the dressing: **two parts rice vinegar to
one part sugar by weight**, dissolved **completely** in cold vinegar and never over heat — grit in the
bottom means the top of the bowl is sour and the last mouthful is sweet.

**MOAT = cucumber's low standing and the Tokugawa crest.** It was grown for a long stretch to ripen soft
and yellow on the vine — sweeter, seedier and by most accounts not very good — and eating it young,
green and firm is the *later* idea that made it worth having. Edo-period writers ranked vegetables and
cucumber sits near the bottom, dismissed as watery and flavourless, *which is precisely why every good
Japanese cucumber dish is a lesson in technique rather than in ingredients.* Then the crest: a cucumber
cut straight across shows three seed chambers inside a circle, close enough to the **mitsuba-aoi**, the
three hollyhock leaves in a ring that was the Tokugawa crest, that samurai loyal to them were reluctant
to eat it in rounds at all — putting a knife through your lord's crest and then chewing it was not a
good look. Rule or etiquette, **the practical effect survives**: Japanese cooking cuts cucumber on the
diagonal, into batons, fans and accordions far more readily than into plain discs, and the cut in this
record is one of that family.
