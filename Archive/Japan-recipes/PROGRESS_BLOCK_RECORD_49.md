**THE LEAD — the one miso soup that starts in a frying pan:**

- Every other miso soup in the file is assembly. **Fry this one first or you get a thin, slightly greasy
  soup with meat in it.** ⚖️ **The fat is not an accident of using pork. The fat is the point.**
- **Render the pork belly in a DRY pan** — no oil at all — from cold, medium heat, 3–4 minutes. Belly is
  roughly half fat and a dry pan gives up a full tablespoon of clear fat plus browned edges, *which is
  where the meat flavour comes from in a soup that simmers only fifteen minutes.* Add oil at the start
  and the fat cannot get out cleanly, so the meat steams instead of colouring. **Do not pour the fat off.**
- **Then fry the vegetables in it for a full 4–5 minutes**, which is the step everyone skips, and it does
  two separate things: the fat coats every cut face so the vegetables release into a broth that already
  has fat to carry the sweetness; and **the pork fat, the vegetable starch and the miso proteins form a
  loose emulsion**, so the fat ends up suspended in droplets through the broth instead of floating as a
  slick. *That is why tonjiru is cloudy and clings to a spoon, and you cannot get there by adding fat at
  the end.*
- **The board decides it, not the pot.** ⚠️ **Daikon is the SLOWEST thing in the soup** → 4–5mm
  quarter-rounds. **Potato is the FASTEST** → generous 3cm chunks, because small pieces dissolve long
  before the daikon softens. Onion in wedges, pork sliced thin so it renders in minutes not twenty.
- **SKIM the grey-brown foam in the first two or three minutes** — aku, thrown by the pork. Stirred back
  in it makes the soup taste muddy and faintly metallic.
- **Miso off the heat**, smoothed in a ladle with hot broth before any of it reaches the pot — a lump
  dropped in does not disperse, it breaks into grit that never dissolves. The *why* is the miso soup
  record's and is **pointed at, not repeated**; the only thing restated is that it is not a matter of
  degree — off the heat, not turned down.
- **Sesame oil is 5ml AT THE END**, never at the start: low smoke point, volatile aroma, and frying in it
  leaves a slightly bitter fat and nothing else.
- ⚖️ **AND THE INVERSION THAT SETS IT APART FROM EVERY OTHER SOUP IN THIS FILE: TONJIRU IS BETTER ON THE
  SECOND DAY.** A plain miso soup is tired within the hour. Overnight the pork fat and the roots go on
  exchanging, potato starch keeps thickening the broth, and the miso's edges round off. Reheat to
  **steaming and no further**, wake it with a dot of fresh miso and new spring onion. *That is not a way
  of using up leftovers — for a great many households it is how the dish is planned.*

**MOAT = THE COMMUNAL POT, and every clause of it is in the recipe.** Tonjiru is the standard dish of the
volunteer relief kitchen: after Kobe in 1995 and across the north-east in 2011, one of the first signs
that help had reached a place was propane burners and dustbin-sized stock pots turning out tonjiru by the
hundred litres. It is also the school sports day, the winter festival, the cherry-blossom party in the
cold week when nobody is warm, and the building site where somebody has to feed thirty people at noon.
**Why it and not something else:** it scales without changing, and nothing in it must be served the
minute it is done · its vegetables survive without refrigeration and travel bruised without complaint ·
pork belly is the most calories per rand and per kilo carried of almost anything you could put in a pot,
which matters when people are cold · miso is salt, protein and savour that keeps a year in a tub and
needs no measuring · **and the dish is better after standing, so a pot made at ten is at its best at
one** — the exact opposite of almost everything else in Japanese cooking, and exactly what you need when
you are cooking for a queue rather than for a table. *Worth knowing that this recipe is a small version
of a big pot, rather than the other way round.*
